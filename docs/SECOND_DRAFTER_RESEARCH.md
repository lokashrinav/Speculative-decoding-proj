# What is published about small second drafters, and what it means for ours

Notes from reading the papers themselves (November 18 and 19, 2026). Each entry says what the method
adds to a block drafter such as DFlash, what is trained and on how much text, what it costs in a
round, and what it gains. The last section is what we take from it. Numbers are the papers' own, on
their own hardware and data; they are not comparable with ours without care.

Words: the *first run* is the drafter's one pass that writes a whole block; a *place* is a position
in that block; the *candidates* of a place are the first run's most probable tokens there; a
*branch* is a path of a tree of drafts; *tokens per round* is the accepted length including the
target's own token.

## Training the first drafter better

- Draft-OPD and MDraft (2610.37778): the drafter is trained on its OWN drafts with the target's
  answer to them. MDraft: +4% tokens per round over the released DFlash for Qwen3-4B, on 667,000
  sequences. (Our better drafter, `bingyang-lei/Qwen3-4B-Thinking-Draft-OPD`, is of this family.)
- D3D (2611.06425): a drafter that needs no target states (a pruned 31-layer model), so that it can
  draft the next round while the target checks. Two GPUs.

## What we take from it (as of November 19, 01:40 UTC; more reading follows below)

1. **A small second drafter on a frozen first drafter can work: Weaver.** It is one layer, as ours.
   It differs from ours in three ways that the other papers confirm matter:
   - *text*: 300,000 answers x 128 positions, against our 366 answers x about 280 rounds;
   - *what it learns from*: the target's own probabilities at every place (distilling the target),
     with the true tokens before the place given. Ours learned the right token, and only at the
     places where the first run broke;
   - *what it scores*: the first run's candidates only (512), never the whole vocabulary.
2. **Every place, true tokens before it.** Domino, DCOCO, DRelay and Weaver all train with the
   true tokens before a place (teacher forcing): a place only matters when everything before it was
   accepted. None trains only at breaks.
3. **Frozen first drafter is the hard case.** DCOCO frozen: +4.4%; DForkTree's head: no more than the
   counts. Weaver is the exception, with far more text and a wider reach (512 candidates, the
   whole branch). So with little text we should not expect Weaver's gain; what we can measure is
   how the gain grows with text, with and without the counts underneath.
4. **Counts first.** They cost nothing and already give +9.6% here. The second drafter should be
   trained ON TOP of them (their correction is in its scores while it trains), so that it learns
   what they do not know.
5. **Cost.** The working heads cost 0.3 to 2 ms a round in compiled servers. Ours is a drafter
   layer (about 1.4 ms here) plus scores over candidates only; it has to stay at one call a round.

## What the reading of November 19 adds

6. **A second pass that reads the draft's tokens works when it is trained for it.** D2SD and D-Loop
   both reveal the TRUE tokens of a prefix whose length is drawn at random (short ones most often)
   and weight the places after it by a decaying cross-entropy. The same drafter without that
   training gives +14% tokens (D2SD's own control), which is what our frozen whole-drafter restart
   showed. Both use about 1.3M prompts and several passes over them, and both pay a WHOLE drafter
   pass. Neither is on a tree: they redraft 4 places of a chain.
2b. Our trained layers so far broke two of these rules: they were trained only at the places where
   the first run broke, and on a few hundred answers.
7. **Most of the gain of "knowing the token before" is in the pair (token before, token).** DSpark's
   plain pair table gets nearly all of what its recurrent head gets; GRAFT's pair scorer gets +6%
   on a frozen drafter with 3,000 prompts; DForkTree's counts are the same information without
   training. A second drafter has to beat pair knowledge, so it must be measured ON TOP of the
   counts, and its training loss should already contain them.
8. **Targets.** DSpark, UBTree, Weaver and GRAFT all train against the target's PROBABILITIES over
   the candidates (total variation or KL), not only its first choice. UBTree shows that labels for
   the second-best branches matter for trees.
9. **What is still not published, as far as this reading goes:** a second pass on a frozen drafter
   that costs one layer because it starts from the first pass's states, used at every node of a
   tree in one batch, on top of the counts, with a measurement of how its gain grows with the
   amount of training text. D2SD, D-Loop and DLoop pay a whole pass and retrain on 1.3M prompts;
   Weaver adds a new layer with 300,000 answers; DForkTree's second pass was trained on 3,000
   answers and did not pay.

## Which loss, when the training text is the target's own greedy text (read November 19, 02:00 UTC)

"Target Regeneration Shrinks the Gains of Draft-Training Objectives" (2610.29013) trains the same
block drafter with three losses on text written by others and on text the target wrote greedily.
On the target's own greedy text, distilling the target's probabilities (its 8 likeliest tokens and
"the rest") adds only +3.7% tokens per round over plain cross-entropy with a tree of 63, +1.0% with
a chain, and +1.0% in its text-only test on Qwen3-4B. A gate that drops places after a token the
target would not write adds nothing there. What is left of the gain "lies in how the other tree
candidates are ranked". The same loss looked worth +14% on text written by others.

For us: our training text is the target's greedy text, so the loss is not the main lever. The
target's probabilities come from the pass that also gives the drafter its context, so distilling
them costs nothing and helps the ranking of second-best branches a little; we use both terms and
do not expect more than a few percent from it. The levers are the ones the working second passes
share: every place with the true tokens before it, enough text, scores over the candidates only.

Also read and set aside: HyFlash (2611.07263) gives a small drafter a recurrent memory of the
target's states of the whole text; DNeo (2611.21925) and DynaDFlash (2611.19085) change how the
target's states are fed to the first drafter; Multi-Future Drafting (2611.13017) lets the first
drafter write several whole futures. All three change the first drafter, not a second one.

## Our second drafter after this reading (built November 19, 02:00 UTC)

What it is. A copy of the drafter's LAST layer with low-rank updates (`dflash/restart_layers.py`).
It does not start over: every place of the block enters with the state the first run had there
before its last layer, and the tokens of a branch are written in at their places. It then gives,
for the place after the branch, a correction of the first run's probabilities of that place's 32
most probable tokens (`CandidateScores`): the first run's log-probability, plus how much the
second drafter's score of the token differs from the first run's, plus the counts' lift with
DForkTree's weights; all other tokens together keep the first run's probability, moved by a number
read from the second drafter's state.

How it is trained (`nexus/train_every.py`), following points 1, 2, 6, 7 and 8 above.

- Every place, true tokens before it. For 256 positions of an answer the first run drafts its
  block; for 4 depths each (shallow ones most often) the answer's own tokens are written in up to
  the depth and the next two places are asked for. All rows of 32 positions run in one pass.
- Candidates only, as in use.
- On top of the counts. The lift is in the scores while it trains. A training answer is scored
  with counts that do not contain it (the answers are split in two halves, each scored with the
  other half's counts), so that it does not learn to trust counts that already know the answer.
- Half of the loss is the target's token, half the target's own probabilities over the same
  candidates.

How it is used (`dflash/redraft.py`: `SecondTree`, method names `stree63@NAME`, `cstree63@NAME`).
A first tree of 63 nodes is built as before (with counts for `cstree`). ONE pass of the second
drafter has a row for every node, with the node's path written in. The tree that the target checks
is then chosen anew, best first, by the corrected probabilities of all edges; a node of the first
tree can fall out and a new token can come in. The first place keeps the first run's
probabilities. Weaver and DARTree walk the tree in several steps one after the other with a new
head; this is one pass of a layer the drafter already has. A test checks that what the pass gives
a node is, number for number, what the training asked for at that place.

What is measured.

1. At the end of every training, on 12 validation answers, at every depth: how often the first
   choice is right at the place after the true tokens, for the first run, the first run with
   counts, the second drafter, and the second drafter with counts; separately after the branch has
   left the first run's first choices ("off", where a tree's branches are). DForkTree measured 18%
   there for the first run and 84 to 89% with the target's real states.
2. The same after training on 100, 300, 1,000 and all (about 2,970) answers, with the counts of the
   same answers: gain against text.
3. Tokens per round in replay for `cstree63` against `ctree63` (the tree with counts, +9.6% over
   DDTree here) and against the tree whose second run reads the target's real states (+17.5%).
   A pass through one layer costs about 2.1 ms of a 72 ms round, so the tree has to hold about 3%
   more tokens than `ctree63` before it is faster.

Jobs (all Draft-OPD): `count-tree-opd-0` trains one layer on top of the counts (saved `opd-e1c`),
`gen-think-0` one layer without counts (`opd-e1`), `every-opd-2c` two layers (`opd-e2c`),
`every-opd-100c`, `-300c`, `-1000c` the curve, `every-replay-opd-0` the replay.

## The control that decides whether this is more than Weaver with less text (queued November 19, 02:50 UTC)

Weaver and ours are the same family: one small layer on a frozen drafter that reads a branch's
tokens and corrects the first run's candidates. What we claim on top is that going on from the
drafter's OWN last layer (and the counts) needs far less text than a new layer. Two controls of
`nexus/train_every.py` test it on the same text, the same rounds and the same loss:

- `--fresh` (job `every-opd-2c`): the layer starts from new weights, all trained; its correction
  is read through a map that starts at nothing, so its scores start as the first run's with the
  counts, as a new head's would.
- `--full` (job `every-opd-full`): the layer starts as the drafter's own, all weights trained in
  place of the low-rank change.

If ours is clearly ahead of `--fresh` on about 3,000 answers, that gap is the result. If it is not,
the method is Weaver's with a different layer, and the paper has no point of its own. The
two-layer training moved to the job `every-opd-2cb`.

First numbers of the trainings, on answers seen for the first time (means over 200 answers, about
730 answers in): after the branch has left the first run's first choices, the first choice at the
next place is right for the first run in 18% of the places, with counts in 35%, for the second
drafter trained without counts in 36%, and for the second drafter on top of the counts in 43%.
The right token is among the 32 candidates in 72% of these places.

## What a pass really costs, and the lean pass (November 19, 03:00 UTC)

The real-speed test with the counts (job `train-more-opd-1`, Draft-OPD, 4 of 8 answers so far; plain
decoding 82.3 tokens a second, a round 69 ms):

| Method | Tokens per round | Time of the tree or second run | Speed against plain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DDTree, 63 nodes | +31.3% | 0.8 ms | +20.8% |
| Tree with counts, 63 nodes | +43.5% | 3.2 ms | +28.2% |
| Tree with counts, 95 nodes | +48.3% | 4.0 ms | +29.0% |
| Tree with counts and in-answer rows | +49.4% | 5.0 ms | +27.8% |
| Old one-layer second run after every node (`opd-r1`) | +35.5% | 10.0 ms | +10.7% |
| Old two-layer second run on the tree with counts (`opd-s2`) | +44.2% | 17.0 ms | +8.8% |
| Tree with counts, drafter reading 4,096 tokens | +51.6% | 3.2 ms | +36.8% |

Two things follow. The counts are worth about 6% in real speed over DDTree here (their lookup and
search cost 2.4 ms in our code). And the old second runs through one and two layers cost 10 and
17 ms, not the 2 to 3.5 ms a layer was estimated at: a pass costs by the PLACES that run through
it, and every node's row held its whole block (up to 16 places a node, 40 nodes), then the whole
vocabulary's output head.

The new tree avoids both. Its scores read 32 rows of the output head a place, not the vocabulary.
And with one layer, the places with tokens written in are only looked at by the places that are
asked for; nothing reads what the layer would make of them. So they are given to the layer as keys
and values only (once per node, shared by the rows below it), and only the 2 places asked for per
node run through the layer: about 126 places in place of up to 1,000. A test checks that the scores
are the same numbers either way. What it costs in real decoding is measured after the replay; until
then the "about 2 ms" for one layer is an estimate, and the old passes show such estimates were low.

What this says.

1. The retraining worked: the old layers added nothing to the tree with counts, this one adds 3.4%,
   which is half of what a second run with the target's real states adds (6.7%).
2. Training on top of the counts is worth 1.4 of the 3.4 points (2.0% when trained without them).
3. The accuracy after a fork rose far more (39% to 50%) than the tokens per round (3.4%). The tree
   with real states (about 85% right at the next place) also adds only 6.7%. So at 63 nodes the
   next place after a fork is not what limits a tree; what follows it is. The second drafter gives a
   node's children their corrected probabilities, but a NEW child has no row of its own: it gets one
   chain token. Levers, cheapest first: a longer chain per row (`--reach 4`, job `every-opd-300c`,
   saved `opd-e1cr4`), 64 candidates (job `every-opd-2cb`, `opd-e1ck64`), a second pass for the new
   nodes, two layers (`every-opd-2cc`).
4. Used alone, the second drafter gives a tree about as good as the counts' tree (+8.5% against
   +9.9%) without any table. Its tree is searched less deeply than the counts' tree (one pass over a
   first tree against a search at every depth), which is the same limit as in point 3.
5. Not known yet: what the pass costs in a round. The real-speed test is the job `every-opd-2c`
   (`plain+cstree63@opd-e1c` against `plain+ctree63` and `plain+tree63`).

Jobs after the reordering of 06:05 UTC: `every-opd-2c` real speed; `every-opd-300c` longer chain;
`every-opd-2cb` 64 candidates; `every-opd-full` the control with a layer from new weights;
`every-opd-300cb` the control with the drafter's layer fully trained; `every-opd-100c` 100 answers;
`every-opd-100cb` 300 answers; `every-opd-1000c` 1,000 answers; `every-opd-2cc` two layers.
`train-more-opd-0` (the old recipe on more text) has hung since about 02:27 UTC at 1,042 answers

## Gaps in this account, and the diagnosis that comes before any new architecture (November 19, 19:55 UTC)

Shrinav had the account above audited and brought back the gaps. The scope from here on is his:
DDTree with a second drafter or a smaller neural network (the count table is a baseline, not the
method). The gaps that hold, and what is done about each:

1. **"Cost is the bottleneck, not accuracy" does not follow from the numbers.** The second drafter
   is right more often than the counts after a fork (49.1% against 39.5% of the places), and its
   tree holds FEWER tokens a round in replay (8.21 against 8.31). Tokens per round do not include
   time, so cost cannot explain that. Read in the code (dflash/redraft.py: SecondTree), the likely
   reason is the tree, not the guess: the tree with counts is searched with its scores at EVERY
   depth, while the second drafter's one pass gives rows to the nodes of the FIRST tree only. A
   token the pass brings in gets no row of its own: after it the tree has at most the one chain
   token of its parent's row. In the letters example the pass puts in g and corrects h; h is new,
   nothing after h is corrected. So the accuracy (measured at every place given the true tokens)
   and the tokens (limited by how far a corrected branch can go) measure different things.
2. **The accuracy was not weighted by what a round reaches.** It is measured at all places of a
   block after the true tokens; a round only reaches the place after the first fork if the tree
   has the fork, and the places after that if the tree has the continuation.
3. **The scores are over the first run's 32 candidates only.** If the answer's token is not among
   them no scorer can pick it (after a fork it is among them in 81.7% of the places, among 64 in
   88.6%). How often a round ends that way was not measured.
4. **The "ceiling" of +6.7% with the target's real states bounds one setup**: rows for a first
   tree's nodes, two places after each, the same re-selection. It does not bound a second drafter
   used at every depth.
5. **Overstated**: that the pair scores "should" be 6% ahead of DDTree with a cheap step (a
   projection from other papers' timings, not measured); that the control with a layer from new
   weights says something about architecture (it says the start of the weights matters little);
   any "nobody has done this" (DARTree, arXiv 2608.13524, corrects the branches of a draft tree
   with a small head).

Already covered: the tokens per round are from replay and real decoding on held-out answers, with
trees built from nothing but the drafter's outputs (the split is by answer); only the accuracy
percentages are with the true tokens given. The step's time is split (pass 3.3 ms, scoring 1.6,
rows and choice 1.4). And the second drafter already works the way one audit point fears: it is
ONE layer that goes on from the first run's old states with the branch's tokens written in; the
layers before it never saw the branch. Whether that holds it back is check 3 below.

**The diagnosis (no architecture work before it).**

- `nexus/trace_trees.py` builds several trees in the SAME rounds (the answer is decoded with the
  first one; every tree gets the same first run) and records for each round and tree how many
  tokens of the answer the tree holds and why the answer's path is not in the tree at the next
  place: block end; the token is not a candidate; no correction left (a node no pass had a row
  for); the chain's one token is wrong; ranked first by the tree's scores but not kept (the nodes
  went elsewhere); ranked 2 to 4; ranked 5 or lower. Each with where it is relative to the first
  fork, how often a tree gets less far than another on the same round, and how many tokens would
  follow if the stop were mended.
- Kept apart from that, "following the answer" (a ceiling, not a method: it knows which branch
  matters): where each tree's scores put the answer's token among the children of the answer's own
  path at every depth, and how far a path gets if the b best children are kept at every node.
- `SecondTree.passes`: after the first pass, the nodes of the tree without a row get one and the
  tree is chosen anew, until no node is left without one (names `stree63p2@…`, `p16`). Built from
  the drafter's outputs only, so it is a deployable rule with the time ignored: what the second
  drafter's scores are worth when the tree's construction does not cut them short.
- Check 3: the same training as `opd-e1` with the drafter's last 2 and last 4 layers run again
  over the branch's tokens (`opd-e2`, `opd-e4`).

Jobs: `diag1-trace` (the trace: DDTree, counts with 16 and 32 candidates, pair scores alone and on
the counts, the second drafter with 1, 2 and all passes, alone and on the counts), `diag2-replay`
(the trees with more passes in replay, each on its own rounds), `diag3-two`, `diag4-four` (check 3,
each followed by its trace). What decides the next step: if the second drafter with a row for
every node holds clearly more tokens than with one pass, the work is how to give every depth its
correction for the price of one pass or less (a table-shaped form, rows chosen better); if most
rounds end at a token that is not a candidate, it is the candidates; if they end at tokens the
scores rank low, it is the predictor (more layers, more of the branch).

## The published second passes made here, and whether a trained pass still needs the states (November 27, 02:00 UTC)

Shrinav's question: the untrained second run is missing the target's hidden states for the new tokens
(0.59 tokens after the break with tokens only, 2.17 with the real states on the public drafter; 0.93
and 3.65 on Draft-OPD). Does a TRAINED second pass, D-Loop's or D2SD's, still gain from them? If it
does, a predictor of the states is a lever for their methods, which pay the pass anyway, and not only
for ours. The literature (two research reports, November 26): DForkTree (2611.12356) states the cause
and measures it as top-1 accuracy (29% to 84% with the target's features, 37 to 39% with tokens
only); JetSpec++ (2610.19792) predicts the target's states for an unverified block with a 12-layer
adapter, for drafting ahead; nobody has measured accepted tokens with and without the states, a
small in-round predictor against the oracle, or whether a trained second pass still gains from the
states. Our trained one-layer second drafter ignores them (0.87 predicted, 0.90 real, 0.86 none);
a trained whole pass is unmeasured. Neither D-Loop nor D2SD has released weights (D2SD's come
"with the camera-ready"), so both are made here (`nexus/train_loop.py`):

- `loop1-d2sd`: a separate whole drafter, started from Draft-OPD, all weights trained to fill the end
  of a block given the true beginning (prefix lengths ~ 0.85^l, cross-entropy decaying from the first
  masked place, tau 7, lr 1e-4, up to 2 passes over long1's training answers and think1, 5 hours).
  D2SD's own recipe is only partly published (beta, tau, lr and the start are not stated); the
  choice follows the research report (full fine-tune, start from the released drafter, a longer
  mean prefix for thinking-mode text). Then the probe with the trained drafter making the restarts
  (nothing / predicted `opd-m0` / real states), and replay of `branches4` (the unchanged drafter as
  the second drafter: D2SD's control, +14% tokens on their 8B) against `branches4@drafter-opd-d2sd`.
- `loop2-dloop`: Draft-OPD itself retrained with the paper's two views (an ordinary block, and a block
  with the true beginning filled in; 0.7^l, decays 7, lr 2e-4), one set of weights for both passes.
  Then the probe and replay with the retrained drafter, and `plain,branches4` with the original for
  the comparison (does the retraining hurt the first pass).

Why D2SD first (the report's reasoning): the first pass stays the released drafter, so every gain is
the second pass's; under greedy decoding D2SD's gain over its own baseline is the larger (+33% tokens,
+20% speed against D-Loop's +18% tokens, +4% speed on Qwen3-4B); retraining the shared weights on
11M tokens could drift a drafter trained on far more. What to expect, as extrapolation only: tokens
between the untrained reuse (+14%) and the full-data result (+33%); speed on a 4B target a few
percent, since D-Loop turned +17.7% tokens into +3.7% speed there. Memory: the 537M-parameter
drafter in 32-bit with AdamW is about 8.6 GB next to the 8 GB target.

## What is new in the win, what is not, and the plan to a publishable number (November 27, 04:30 UTC)

A reading of the 2026 trees and correctors (DForkTree 2611.12356 v2, GRAFT 2608.20375, UBTree 2609.39972,
Weaver 2607.06763, CPA 2611.31112, TreeSpark 2609.22098 and 2610.20175, DSpark 2607.05147, the DFlash 2
selector, "Beyond Parallel Blindness" 2608.27339, D2SD 2606.04446, "Give the Drafter a Second Chance"
2611.05724) against the win above.

**New, as far as the reading found.** No published corrector is told, as a number, how sure the first run
was of the token before a place. The nearest is DForkTree's pair of fixed weights (0.3 on the path of first
choices, 0.6 off it) and the on-path flag its distilled head reads; GRAFT scales its correction by one
constant; UBTree scales by depth; the DFlash 2 selector drops the tail mass; CPA starts its corrections at
zero. All of these are fixed structure, none a learned input. DForkTree's own appendix reports the fact
that makes the input worth having: right after a fork the drafter's accuracy "rises from 18% to 48% with
the marginal probability of the fork token", and it did not feed that probability to its head. Our gain is
also larger than fork accuracy explains: +5.1 points right after a fork gave +0.45 tokens a round (+5.5%),
where DForkTree's rule of thumb (five points of closure, about 1%) predicts a fifth of that; the four-layer
second drafter is more accurate at the fork (57.3%) and holds fewer tokens (8.49 against 8.65); and 18% of
our stops were "ranked first, the node budget went elsewhere". So the three numbers most likely fix the
scores' calibration between on-path and off-path nodes (DForkTree's Table 12: on-path nodes accepted 1.20
times what their scores say, off-path 0.71 times) and the budget's allocation, not only the ranking at the
fork. That is a hypothesis to test with the ablations below, and the paper's claim if it holds.

**Not new.** Distilling a re-anchored (second) drafter into a cheap head: DForkTree's appendix does it
(its head closes 34–38% of the gap after a fork, 41–49% with the PMI as input), and reports that the
drafter's hidden state is worth 1–2 points to that head. It never compared the head with the same head
trained on the target's own labels; that comparison is open. A second pass through the drafter not paying
for itself: DForkTree's LoRA second pass (+1–2.9% tokens, 2.5–6.2 ms with CUDA graphs) and our experiment 3.
One token before a place carrying most of the information: 2608.27339 (one realised token removes 86–100%
of the floor); the second token helps, a third does not (DForkTree Tables 2 and 9); attention over the whole
branch is worth about +5% tokens / +4.8% speed in CPA, mostly on chat text.

**The bar.** Over DDTree with the same drafter, published: DForkTree +15.1% throughput on Qwen3.5-4B,
UBTree +25.3% on thinking Qwen3-4B with a retrained drafter, Weaver +6–15%, TreeSpark +9–16% tokens. Ours:
+9.1% real speed (1.325 / 1.214) on 2 answers, eager timing; +14.4% tokens a round on 8 (8.65 / 7.56). The
corrector's cost is the gap: 4.3 ms more than DDTree a round here, where GRAFT's edge scorer costs 0.3 ms,
DForkTree's whole tree build 0.9 ms, DSpark's Markov head 0.2–1.3% of a round. At the same 8.14 tokens a
round, a corrector of 0.8 ms would make the round about 71 ms and the gain about +15% over DDTree. A
workshop paper needs: graphed real speed ≥ +12–15% over DDTree on the 7-benchmark suite of DFlash/DDTree,
a clear +3–5% over the count table on the same drafter, the corrector ≤ 1 ms, and the ablation that says
where the gain comes from.

**What the trace says the three numbers do (next1-trace, 6,993 rounds of DDTree, 7 answers, read 04:05 UTC).**
Why the path stops, `opd-p2` against `opd-p2s`: right token ranked 5th or lower 28.3% → 24.9%; ranked 2nd to
4th 38.4% → 38.6%; ranked first but the node budget went elsewhere 13.5% → 12.5%; block's end 9.9% → 11.9%;
not among the 32 candidates 9.9% → 12.1%. Against DDTree on the same rounds: `opd-p2` further in 41.9% of
rounds and less far in 10.3% (gaining 0.87 tokens a round, losing 0.18); `opd-p2s` further in 49.4%, less far
in 4.5% (gaining 1.16, losing 0.06). Keeping only the corrector's first choice at every node: DDTree 6.00,
`opd-p2` 6.03, `opd-p2s` 6.39. So the calibration hypothesis above is mostly wrong: the "budget went
elsewhere" stops hardly moved. About 70% of the gain is better ranking (tokens that were ranked 5th or lower
now ranked first) and 30% is less damage to DDTree's good rounds. Both fit one mechanism: the numbers set the
strength of the correction, large after an unexpected token and small after an expected one, where the scores
without them had to use one average strength. The ablation that decides the claim is `abl1-top1`: if the flag
alone (two strengths) reaches 8.5 or more of the 8.63, the idea is DForkTree's weights learned; if 8.4 or less,
the graded number does the work. The 12.5% of stops "ranked first, budget went elsewhere" is untouched by every
corrector here and would be worth 0.28 tokens a round if mended: a calibration of scores across depths and
on/off the path, a few learned temperatures on top of `opd-p2s`, is the cheapest next lever.
