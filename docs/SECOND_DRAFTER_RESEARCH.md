# What is published about small second drafters, and what it means for ours

Notes from reading the papers themselves (November 18 and 19, 2026). Each entry says what the method
adds to a block drafter such as DFlash, what is trained and on how much text, what it costs in a
round, and what it gains. The last section is what we take from it. Numbers are the papers' own, on
their own hardware and data; they are not comparable with ours without care.

Words: the *first run* is the drafter's one pass that writes a whole block; a *place* is a position
in that block; the *candidates* of a place are the first run's most probable tokens there; a
*branch* is a path of a tree of drafts; *tokens per round* is the accepted length including the
target's own token.

## Training the first drafter better

- Draft-OPD and MDraft (2610.37778): the drafter is trained on its OWN drafts with the target's
  answer to them. MDraft: +4% tokens per round over the released DFlash for Qwen3-4B, on 667,000
  sequences. (Our better drafter, `bingyang-lei/Qwen3-4B-Thinking-Draft-OPD`, is of this family.)
- D3D (2611.06425): a drafter that needs no target states (a pruned 31-layer model), so that it can
  draft the next round while the target checks. Two GPUs.

## What we take from it (as of November 19, 01:40 UTC; more reading follows below)

1. **A small second drafter on a frozen first drafter can work: Weaver.** It is one layer, as ours.
   It differs from ours in three ways that the other papers confirm matter:
   - *text*: 300,000 answers x 128 positions, against our 366 answers x about 280 rounds;
   - *what it learns from*: the target's own probabilities at every place (distilling the target),
     with the true tokens before the place given. Ours learned the right token, and only at the
     places where the first run broke;
   - *what it scores*: the first run's candidates only (512), never the whole vocabulary.
2. **Every place, true tokens before it.** Domino, DCOCO, DRelay and Weaver all train with the
   true tokens before a place (teacher forcing): a place only matters when everything before it was
   accepted. None trains only at breaks.
3. **Frozen first drafter is the hard case.** DCOCO frozen: +4.4%; DForkTree's head: no more than the
   counts. Weaver is the exception, with far more text and a wider reach (512 candidates, the
   whole branch). So with little text we should not expect Weaver's gain; what we can measure is
   how the gain grows with text, with and without the counts underneath.
4. **Counts first.** They cost nothing and already give +9.6% here. The second drafter should be
   trained ON TOP of them (their correction is in its scores while it trains), so that it learns
   what they do not know.
5. **Cost.** The working heads cost 0.3 to 2 ms a round in compiled servers. Ours is a drafter
   layer (about 1.4 ms here) plus scores over candidates only; it has to stay at one call a round.

## What the reading of November 19 adds

6. **A second pass that reads the draft's tokens works when it is trained for it.** D2SD and D-Loop
   both reveal the TRUE tokens of a prefix whose length is drawn at random (short ones most often)
   and weight the places after it by a decaying cross-entropy. The same drafter without that
   training gives +14% tokens (D2SD's own control), which is what our frozen whole-drafter restart
   showed. Both use about 1.3M prompts and several passes over them, and both pay a WHOLE drafter
   pass. Neither is on a tree: they redraft 4 places of a chain.
2b. Our trained layers so far broke two of these rules: they were trained only at the places where
   the first run broke, and on a few hundred answers.
7. **Most of the gain of "knowing the token before" is in the pair (token before, token).** DSpark's
   plain pair table gets nearly all of what its recurrent head gets; GRAFT's pair scorer gets +6%
   on a frozen drafter with 3,000 prompts; DForkTree's counts are the same information without
   training. A second drafter has to beat pair knowledge, so it must be measured ON TOP of the
   counts, and its training loss should already contain them.
8. **Targets.** DSpark, UBTree, Weaver and GRAFT all train against the target's PROBABILITIES over
   the candidates (total variation or KL), not only its first choice. UBTree shows that labels for
   the second-best branches matter for trees.
9. **What is still not published, as far as this reading goes:** a second pass on a frozen drafter
   that costs one layer because it starts from the first pass's states, used at every node of a
   tree in one batch, on top of the counts, with a measurement of how its gain grows with the
   amount of training text. D2SD, D-Loop and DLoop pay a whole pass and retrain on 1.3M prompts;
   Weaver adds a new layer with 300,000 answers; DForkTree's second pass was trained on 3,000
   answers and did not pay.

## Which loss, when the training text is the target's own greedy text (read November 19, 02:00 UTC)

"Target Regeneration Shrinks the Gains of Draft-Training Objectives" (2610.29013) trains the same
block drafter with three losses on text written by others and on text the target wrote greedily.
On the target's own greedy text, distilling the target's probabilities (its 8 likeliest tokens and
"the rest") adds only +3.7% tokens per round over plain cross-entropy with a tree of 63, +1.0% with
a chain, and +1.0% in its text-only test on Qwen3-4B. A gate that drops places after a token the
target would not write adds nothing there. What is left of the gain "lies in how the other tree
candidates are ranked". The same loss looked worth +14% on text written by others.

For us: our training text is the target's greedy text, so the loss is not the main lever. The
target's probabilities come from the pass that also gives the drafter its context, so distilling
them costs nothing and helps the ranking of second-best branches a little; we use both terms and
do not expect more than a few percent from it. The levers are the ones the working second passes
share: every place with the true tokens before it, enough text, scores over the candidates only.

Also read and set aside: HyFlash (2611.07263) gives a small drafter a recurrent memory of the
target's states of the whole text; DNeo (2611.21925) and DynaDFlash (2611.19085) change how the
target's states are fed to the first drafter; Multi-Future Drafting (2611.13017) lets the first
drafter write several whole futures. All three change the first drafter, not a second one.

## Our second drafter after this reading (built November 19, 02:00 UTC)

What it is. A copy of the drafter's LAST layer with low-rank updates (`dflash/restart_layers.py`).
It does not start over: every place of the block enters with the state the first run had there
before its last layer, and the tokens of a branch are written in at their places. It then gives,
for the place after the branch, a correction of the first run's probabilities of that place's 32
most probable tokens (`CandidateScores`): the first run's log-probability, plus how much the
second drafter's score of the token differs from the first run's, plus the counts' lift with
DForkTree's weights; all other tokens together keep the first run's probability, moved by a number
read from the second drafter's state.

How it is trained (`nexus/train_every.py`), following points 1, 2, 6, 7 and 8 above.

- Every place, true tokens before it. For 256 positions of an answer the first run drafts its
  block; for 4 depths each (shallow ones most often) the answer's own tokens are written in up to
  the depth and the next two places are asked for. All rows of 32 positions run in one pass.
- Candidates only, as in use.
- On top of the counts. The lift is in the scores while it trains. A training answer is scored
  with counts that do not contain it (the answers are split in two halves, each scored with the
  other half's counts), so that it does not learn to trust counts that already know the answer.
- Half of the loss is the target's token, half the target's own probabilities over the same
  candidates.

How it is used (`dflash/redraft.py`: `SecondTree`, method names `stree63@NAME`, `cstree63@NAME`).
A first tree of 63 nodes is built as before (with counts for `cstree`). ONE pass of the second
drafter has a row for every node, with the node's path written in. The tree that the target checks
is then chosen anew, best first, by the corrected probabilities of all edges; a node of the first
tree can fall out and a new token can come in. The first place keeps the first run's
probabilities. Weaver and DARTree walk the tree in several steps one after the other with a new
head; this is one pass of a layer the drafter already has. A test checks that what the pass gives
a node is, number for number, what the training asked for at that place.

What is measured.

1. At the end of every training, on 12 validation answers, at every depth: how often the first
   choice is right at the place after the true tokens, for the first run, the first run with
   counts, the second drafter, and the second drafter with counts; separately after the branch has
   left the first run's first choices ("off", where a tree's branches are). DForkTree measured 18%
   there for the first run and 84 to 89% with the target's real states.
2. The same after training on 100, 300, 1,000 and all (about 2,970) answers, with the counts of the
   same answers: gain against text.
3. Tokens per round in replay for `cstree63` against `ctree63` (the tree with counts, +9.6% over
   DDTree here) and against the tree whose second run reads the target's real states (+17.5%).
   A pass through one layer costs about 2.1 ms of a 72 ms round, so the tree has to hold about 3%
   more tokens than `ctree63` before it is faster.

Jobs (all Draft-OPD): `count-tree-opd-0` trains one layer on top of the counts (saved `opd-e1c`),
`gen-think-0` one layer without counts (`opd-e1`), `every-opd-2c` two layers (`opd-e2c`),
`every-opd-100c`, `-300c`, `-1000c` the curve, `every-replay-opd-0` the replay.
